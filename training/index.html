Language practice 8 decks · 7 languages · free, no account

Decks I built to study with

Eight flashcard apps written for my own language study and kept public. Each runs entirely in the browser: progress is stored on your own device, nothing is sent anywhere, and none of them needs an account.

All of them teach a language to Indonesian speakers, so their interface and every translation on the cards are in Indonesian.

Japanese

  • 469 cards · 18 situations

    会話 · Kaiwa

    Everyday conversation: convenience stores, trains, clinics, city offices, banks, the post office, haircuts and the workplace. Includes speech playback and a listening mode that hides the text.

    Search · Audio · Speaker/listener filter
  • 56 cards · 9 categories

    敬語 · Keigo

    Honorific speech for working life, separated into 尊敬語 (sonkeigo), 謙譲語 (kenjōgo) and 丁寧語 (teineigo), plus cards for the mistakes people actually make.

    Verb pairs · Email · Phone · Apologies

Starting from zero

Six decks for languages I started from nothing. Four begin with the writing system itself. Arabic assumes you already read the script, and French, already in Latin letters, carries IPA on every word instead, because its spelling is not read as written.

  • 5 modules · 188 vocabulary entries

    الفصحى · Arabic

    Modern Standard Arabic for readers who already know the script and its vowel marks but not yet the words. Vocabulary, grammar, root families, and reading text stripped of its harakat.

    Leitner boxes · Root patterns
  • 6 modules · 180 vocabulary entries

    한국어 · Korean

    Starts with all 40 Hangeul letters and the batchim groups, then vocabulary, 12 grammar topics, two number systems with their counters, pronunciation rules and the speech levels.

    Hangeul · Counters · Speech levels
  • 6 modules · 209 vocabulary entries

    Русский · Russian

    The 33 Cyrillic letters first, then stress-marked vocabulary, 14 grammar topics, root families with their affixes, pronunciation, and the false friends that catch Indonesian speakers out.

    Cyrillic · Word stress · False friends
  • 8 modules · 502 vocabulary entries

    ภาษาไทย · Thai

    The hardest script here: 44 consonants and their classes, 31 vowel patterns including the ones that are never written, and the five tones with the rules that decide them. Plus ear training on minimal pairs.

    Tone rules · Ear training · Sanskrit cognates
  • 6 modules · 475 vocabulary entries

    Türkçe · Turkish

    Latin script, so the work is elsewhere: vowel harmony and the suffix families built on it, 15 grammar topics, everyday phrases, and the loanwords that look Indonesian but are not.

    Vowel harmony · Suffixes · Loanword traps
  • 5 modules · 159 vocabulary entries

    Le français · French

    Aimed at the three things Indonesian has no equivalent for: spelling that is not read as written, so every word carries IPA and an Indonesian-style respelling; gender on every noun; and verbs that change with the subject. 30 verbs in 4 groups, 18 spelling rules, 12 grammar topics.

    IPA · Gender drill · Faux amis

How they work

  • Progress stays on your device. Cards you mark as learned are remembered in the browser's own storage. There is no sign-in, no server and nothing to delete later.
  • The training wheels come off. Japanese furigana sits above the kanji as real <ruby> markup and can be switched off; the Arabic deck hides harakat the same way, turning any vocabulary card into a reading exercise.
  • Script before words. Four of the newer decks open with the writing system — Hangeul, Cyrillic, Thai consonant classes, the Turkish alphabet — because guessing at letters makes everything after it slower.
  • Built to be read aloud. Kaiwa speaks each line with the browser's Japanese voice, at normal or slow speed.
  • Fast and offline-friendly. Themes are drawn purely in CSS and the data loads from plain scripts, so the decks work from a local file and keep working once cached.

A caveat on the six newer decks

Arabic, Korean, Russian, Thai, Turkish and French were drafted with a language model and checked automatically only on what a machine can check. The language itself has not been reviewed by a native speaker or a teacher, so treat them as study material to verify against a dictionary rather than as a reference. Each repository lists openly the items I am unsure about.
